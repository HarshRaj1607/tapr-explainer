a gentle walk, top to bottom

Teaching a model to reason, not just to answer

RL, LoRA, GRPO, and a question that's still open in research — explained from scratch, in the order they'd actually click for you.

1
the problem

We usually only grade the destination

When a model is trained with reinforcement learning to solve problems, the easiest thing to check is: did it get the right final answer? That single check is called the outcome reward — one number, at the very end.

every step is invisible to the reward — only the last dot counts

This causes two odd things: a model can stumble through nonsense steps, get lucky, and land on the right number — and get full credit. Or it can reason beautifully and slip on one last calculation — and get zero credit. The reward can't tell the difference between "reasoned well" and "got lucky."

2
the basic idea underneath all of this — reinforcement learning

Try, get scored, adjust, try again

Strip away the jargon and reinforcement learning (RL) is a loop you already know from learning any skill: you try something, someone or something tells you how good that was (a reward), and you adjust so next time leans toward what worked.

the model nudges itself toward whatever earned the higher reward

Everything else in this page — LoRA, GRPO, process rewards — is just a more careful way of running this same loop: who gets scored, when, and how the score is used to nudge the model.

3
how you "nudge" a giant model cheaply — LoRA

Don't repaint the whole building — clip on small panels

Modern language models have billions of numbers ("weights"). Adjusting all of them every time you want to try an idea is slow and expensive. LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) freezes the giant model as-is and adds a much smaller set of trainable numbers alongside it — like clipping small, swappable panels onto a building instead of repainting it.

only the small adapter is trained — cheap enough to try many ideas

Why it matters here: it means you can experiment with different rewards and training setups without retraining a whole model from scratch every time — which is exactly what makes checkpoints like the one below affordable to run as a student.

4
how the reward actually gets used — GRPO

Judge attempts against their siblings, not an absolute scale

Give the model the same question several times, so it produces a small group of different attempts. Score each one, then compare each attempt to the group's average — did it do better or worse than its siblings on the exact same problem? That comparison, rather than a raw score, is what pushes the model to change. This is GRPO — Group Relative Policy Optimization.

B and D beat the group average and get reinforced · A and C fall below it and get discouraged

The advantage of doing it this way: you never need to know what a "good" absolute score looks like in isolation — you only need several attempts at the same problem to compare against each other.

5
back to the original problem — grading every step

What if we scored each step, not just the ending?

A process reward looks at the reasoning chain itself, not just where it lands. The simplest version — an isolated step reward — takes each step on its own and asks: is this one statement correct, by itself?

each dot is judged completely on its own — no memory of the others

This already catches more than an outcome-only reward: a step with bad arithmetic gets caught right where it happens, instead of only at the very end.

6
the blind spot

Every step can look fine — and the chain still goes nowhere

Because an isolated judge never sees what came before, it can't notice when a step is technically correct but disconnected from the actual problem — repeating itself, drifting sideways, or quietly answering a different question. Locally each move looks clean. Read top to bottom, the reasoning is lost.

the isolated judge gave this chain full marks, step by step, the whole way

This is the exact gap a checkpoint of undergrad research on this page is trying to close.

7
the idea — TAPR

Score the move, given everything said so far

A transition reward shows the judge the whole chain up to that point, then asks a sharper question: given all of this, does the next move actually follow? Not "is this sentence true on its own" — but "does this specific move earn its place in this specific chain?" That's TAPR: Transition-Aware Process Reward.

the judge now sees the dashed line back to every earlier step before scoring the next one
Where this actually stands: the first real test of this idea produced a null result — the transition judge didn't clearly beat the simple one yet. That's not a failure of the page you're reading, it's what an honest first checkpoint in research usually looks like: a flawed test, a real lesson about how to test it properly, and a clearer next experiment.
